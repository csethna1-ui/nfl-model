# Experiment 008 — Architecture Comparison

**Date:** 2026-09-29 | Target: home margin M = S_h − S_a (home score minus away score)
All architectures are evaluated against V1 (direct scalar regression of margin on ELO+EPA+GBM ensemble) on the same question: does changing the *target/architecture* beat E[margin] estimated directly?

Legend: **Q(A)** = does it improve expected-margin (point) prediction? **Q(B)** = does it improve uncertainty representation? (A model that improves only calibration is a distributional improvement, NOT a superior margin predictor.)

---

## A. Direct margin regression (V1 baseline)

- **Assumptions:** E[M | features] is the object of interest; residual shape irrelevant for the point forecast; OLS/GBM consistent for the conditional mean.
- **Target:** margin directly.
- **Required data:** game margins + pregame features (have: nflverse 2018+).
- **Leakage risks:** features only — standard walk-forward discipline already in place.
- **Home/away dependence:** not represented (single process).
- **Overdispersion:** n/a (not a count model).
- **Game-level variance:** not modeled (implicit constant σ; residual SD ≈ 13).
- **Margin derived:** direct prediction. **Uncertainty derived:** residual SD, empirical.
- **Cost:** trivial. **Walk-forward:** proven (07 script).
- This is the gate every candidate must beat.

## B. Separate home/away score models (independent regressions)

- **Assumptions:** S_h and S_a are each conditionally predictable from (own offense + opponent defense + venue); independence is *not* required for the margin point forecast since E[M] = E[S_h] − E[S_a] exactly.
- **Target:** S_h and S_a separately; margin = difference.
- **Required data:** game-level scores (free, nflverse back to 1999).
- **Feasibility:** full. Same feature pipeline, run twice (home-role and away-role frames).
- **Leakage risks:** same as V1 (features only). No new risk.
- **Home/away dependence:** assumed away in fitting, but dependence only affects *uncertainty* (Var(M) = Var(S_h)+Var(S_a)−2Cov), not the mean margin). Independence costs nothing on Q(A); Q(B) needs a covariance fix (see E).
- **Overdispersion:** mild issue if linear; bounded away if fit on log or via count models.
- **Game-level variance:** not modeled by default.
- **Margin derived:** Ŝ_h − Ŝ_a (mean-of-difference identity holds exactly). For MAE-optimal forecasts, use the conditional median of the *difference*: simulate from the two predictive distributions or use the analytic median of the convolved/score-difference distribution — the median of a difference is NOT the difference of medians in general, so a naive median subtraction is wrong; simulation fixes it cheaply.
- **Uncertainty derived:** bootstrap/simulate pairs, or assume independence + empirical residuals.
- **Cost:** 2× V1 fitting cost. **Walk-forward:** straightforward.
- **Why it matters:** lets offense and defense enter *asymmetrically* (e.g., elite offense vs weak defense explodes the team's own score more than it moves the margin through the opponent's score), which direct margin regression forces into a single signed sum. This is the purest test of 008's "two processes" hypothesis.

## C. Poisson-type scoring models (log-link count regression per team score)

- **Assumptions:** team points ≈ counts generated by a Poisson process with log-rate linear in features.
- **Target:** λ_h, λ_a (expected scores); margin = λ_h − λ_a (Skellam mean identity).
- **Required data:** scores only.
- **Feasibility:** full, but **model mismatch is the problem**: NFL points are not unit events — a 27-point game might be 4 TDs or 9 FGs; the count structure is over event *points*, not events. Raw Poisson on points will mis-specify both mean-variance (variance = mean) and tail shape.
- **Leakage:** features only. **Dependence:** none modeled (independent Poissons → Skellam margin, wrong covariance). **Overdispersion:** structurally *denied* (variance = mean); will fail the variance-predictability diagnostic. **Game-level variance:** fixed by λ.
- **Margin derived:** λ_h − λ_a; median of Skellam via PMF (computable via modified Bessel, but SciPy has no Skellam — implement manually). **Uncertainty:** full Skellam PMF.
- **Cost:** cheap (GLM). **Walk-forward:** easy.
- Verdict at review time: dominated by D on every count-model ground; only reason to keep is as a strawman baseline. Not recommended alone.

## D. Negative-binomial approaches (overdispersion)

- **Assumptions:** S_h, S_a are overdispersed counts: Var > E, dispersion parameter κ either constant or feature-modeled.
- **Target:** (μ, κ) per team; margin = μ_h − μ_a.
- **Required data:** scores only.
- **Feasibility:** full. NB-GLM via statsmodels is off-the-shelf.
- **Leakage:** features only. **Dependence:** none (independent NB → no closed-form margin PMF; simulate). **Overdispersion:** *the* thing it represents — fits the empirical finding that NFL scores are more variable than Poisson. **Game-level variance:** per-team dispersion, feature-modelable.
- **Margin derived:** μ_h − μ_a; MAE-optimal median via simulation of the fitted NB pair. **Uncertainty:** simulated predictive distribution.
- **Cost:** cheap-moderate. **Walk-forward:** easy.
- Honest limitation: NB fixes the *marginal* variance but the events-to-points mismatch (7/3/2/1) remains; it's a better-shaped approximation, not the true generative process. Its value vs B is whether the discrete/overdispersed shape moves the *median of the difference* — testable, not assumed.

## E. Bivariate Poisson / correlated count models (Dixon–Coles style)

- **Assumptions:** S_h, S_a jointly Poisson with a dependence mechanism (common-shock term à la Karlis & Ntzoufras, or copula à la McHale & Scarf 2011).
- **Target:** joint PMF of (S_h, S_a).
- **Required data:** scores only.
- **Feasibility:** full, but fitting is fiddly (bespoke likelihoods; no off-the-shelf NFL implementation). Dependence parameter estimable by MLE on ~3000 games.
- **Leakage:** features only — but *temporal leakage trap*: the Dixon–Coles dynamic form updates strengths on past games; must keep the weekly freeze discipline.
- **Dependence:** explicitly represented — the only family here that does. **Overdispersion:** still denied within Poisson marginals (need bivariate NB extension for both).
- **Game-level variance:** via rates + dependence.
- **Margin derived:** simulate joint → median of M. **Uncertainty:** full joint predictive.
- **Cost:** moderate (custom likelihood, ~100 walk-forward refits fine).
- Literature note: in soccer, Dixon–Coles found dependence empirically *necessary* (ad hoc correction). No published NFL estimate of the home/away score dependence sign/magnitude was found — Phase 2 must measure it before this architecture earns its complexity. If measured dependence ≈ 0, this collapses to C (strawman) and should be dropped.

## F. Distributional regression (GAMLSS-style: location, scale, shape separately)

- **Assumptions:** M | x follows a parametric family (e.g., normal, skew-normal, t) whose location μ(x), scale σ(x), and shape ν(x) are separate regression functions.
- **Target:** full conditional distribution of margin directly.
- **Required data:** margins + features (have everything).
- **Feasibility:** partial — gamlss is R; Python has `gamlss`-incomplete ports; a manual PyTorch/GLM implementation is doable but bespoke. Feature effects on shape parameters are data-hungry at n≈3000.
- **Leakage:** features only. **Dependence:** n/a (margin-level). **Overdispersion:** shape parameter can capture kurtosis/mass at 3 & 7 better than normal. **Game-level variance:** σ(x) modeled — same as G for variance.
- **Margin derived:** μ(x) (mean) or median of fitted family (MAE-optimal). **Uncertainty:** full parametric predictive.
- **Cost:** moderate-high (custom fitting, convergence risk × 100 refits).
- Honest position: strictly more general than G, but the shape parameters are the riskiest to estimate pregame with 3000 games; the incremental value over (G + empirical residual shape) is unproven. Park unless G shows variance is predictable.

## G. Heteroskedastic regression (mean + conditional variance from pregame info)

- **Assumptions:** M | x ~ N(μ(x), σ²(x)); pregame features predict *both* location and scale. (In-game precedent: Duke 2020 thesis models Δscore ~ N(μ(x), σ²(x)) with both regressed on state features.)
- **Target:** (μ, σ) per game.
- **Required data:** margins + features (have everything).
- **Feasibility:** full — two-step (fit mean, regress |residuals|/log σ² on features), or joint MLE; trivially implementable with existing pipeline.
- **Leakage:** features only. **Dependence:** n/a. **Overdispersion:** n/a. **Game-level variance:** *the* object of study.
- **Margin derived:** μ(x) — identical point-forecast target to V1. **Uncertainty:** σ(x) → calibrated intervals, cover probabilities.
- **Cost:** ~1.2× V1. **Walk-forward:** trivial.
- **Critical, non-negotiable fact: heteroskedasticity alone CANNOT improve a point forecast.** The MAE-optimal forecast is the conditional median; for symmetric errors the median = mean = μ(x) regardless of σ(x). If G wins on MAE, it is because its *location* model differs (then it's not really G anymore — it's a location improvement). G's legitimate win condition is Q(B): log-loss / interval calibration / cover-probability accuracy. The 008 directive already requires the **variance-predictability diagnostic FIRST** (if σ(x) is unpredictable pregame, de-emphasize variance architectures) — G is that diagnostic's natural formalization.

## H. Quantile regression (conditional quantiles; median as MAE-optimal point forecast)

- **Assumptions:** conditional quantiles Q_τ(M | x) are linear (or GBM) in features; pinball loss consistent for each τ.
- **Target:** Q_0.25, Q_0.5, Q_0.75 of margin. **The median (τ=0.5) is the MAE-optimal point forecast by construction.**
- **Required data:** margins + features (have everything).
- **Feasibility:** full — LightGBM/GBM with quantile objective; same walk-forward loop as V1; ~3× fitting cost (one model per τ, or multi-output).
- **Leakage:** features only. **Dependence:** n/a. **Overdispersion:** n/a. **Game-level variance:** Q_0.75 − Q_0.25 gives a distribution-free interval; asymmetry (median vs midhinge) captures skew without parametric assumptions.
- **Margin derived:** Q_0.5(x) directly — *a different, MAE-targeted location estimate than V1's conditional mean*. Even with identical features, the median fit can beat a mean fit on MAE under asymmetric/residual-heavy noise. **Uncertainty:** inter-quantile ranges.
- **Cost:** moderate (3 quantile models). **Walk-forward:** straightforward.
- **Risks:** quantile crossing (enforce monotonicity post-hoc or via joint loss); tail quantiles (0.1/0.9) unstable at n≈3000 — restrict to 0.25/0.5/0.75. Published NFL precedent: margin-of-victory quantiles stratified by spread (PLOS ONE 10.1371/journal.pone.0287601).
- This is the *only* architecture on this list whose point-forecast target is definitionally MAE-optimal rather than mean-optimal. If anything beats V1 on MAE with the same features, the prior is here.

## I. Two-stage scoring-process models (drives → points, then aggregate)

- **Assumptions:** drives are the atomic scoring process: model P(points | drive state) and drive count/field position per team, then aggregate to game scores.
- **Target:** drive-level outcomes → simulated/aggregated game margin.
- **Required data:** play-by-play with drive structure (nflverse has it, 1999+). Needs pregame drive-rate features (drives/game, starting field position, red-zone rates).
- **Feasibility:** heavy build. Pregame drive-count forecasting is itself a model; game-script feedback (leading teams change drive behavior) is endogenous.
- **Leakage risks:** HIGH — drive features must be strictly pregame-cutoff; in-game-aggregated drive stats (e.g., season red-zone rate *including* future games) are the classic leak. Also PFR-vs-PBP timing issues documented in the repo.
- **Dependence:** naturally represented via game script (possession alternation, clock). **Overdispersion:** naturally represented (mixture over drive outcomes). **Game-level variance:** via simulation.
- **Margin derived:** simulate N game replicates → median of M. **Uncertainty:** full simulated distribution — best-in-class for Q(B).
- **Cost:** high (two model layers + simulation × 100 refits).
- Honest position: the most *faithful* to the "two scoring processes" premise (Baker & McHale 2013's point-process NFL model is the published cousin and matched the market on exact scores), but it is a large build whose Q(A) payoff is speculative — expected-margin gains must come from a better median, and nothing in the drive structure obviously sharpens the median beyond what B (team-score regression) captures. Build only if B shows the two-process split has legs.

## J. Credible NFL-specific approaches found in the literature

- **Glickman & Stern (1998) dynamic state-space on scores:** Bayesian AR(1) team strengths on *game scores* (not margins), home-field term, MCMC. Strengths: principled time-varying ratings, uncertainty propagation. Weakness for 008: MCMC × ~100 walk-forward refits is expensive; and it's a *rating* system (one strength per team), still estimating E[margin] through team-strength differences — arguably the same target family as V1 with better dynamics. Not a distinct 008 candidate; dynamics already partially covered by ELO.
- **Massey least-squares ratings:** solves for expected differential directly — same target as V1, included for completeness only.
- **Baker & McHale (2013) point-process exact-score model:** the published "two processes" model that matched the betting market on exact scores. Validates the premise; but exact-score log-loss is a different objective than margin MAE, and the implementation is a full research build (see I).
- **Skellam/Poisson-difference:** mathematically clean for unit-point sports; awkward for NFL's 7/3/2/1 scoring. Reference only.
- **Practitioner heteroskedastic normal (Duke 2020 in-game model):** precedent for G.

---

## Recommendation: build at most three

Ranked by expected information per unit of build cost, feasibility with existing free data, and direct bearing on Q(A) vs Q(B):

### 1. H — Quantile regression (τ = 0.25 / 0.5 / 0.75), median as the MAE point forecast
The single strongest theoretical reason to expect a margin-prediction gain: for MAE evaluation the optimal forecast is the conditional median, and V1 estimates the conditional *mean*. Quantile regression changes the estimation target itself, so it can beat V1 on MAE with identical features — no other cheap architecture has this property. Same feature pipeline, same walk-forward loop (LightGBM quantile objective), moderate cost. It also answers Q(B) for free: Q_0.75 − Q_0.25 is a distribution-free prediction interval that can be graded on calibration. Preregister: compare median-MAE vs V1 on 2018–20 dev / 2021–22 validation; separately grade interval calibration. Guard against quantile crossing (post-hoc sort) and restrict to central quantiles at n≈3000.

### 2. B — Separate home/away score models (independent regressions, margin = difference)
The direct test of 008's core hypothesis: margin is the difference of two processes, so estimate the two processes. It lets offense/defense enter asymmetrically (a great offense vs a bad defense moves S_h more than it moves M through S_a), which margin regression structurally cannot express. Needs zero new data, 2× V1 cost, trivial walk-forward. The MAE-optimal point forecast is the median of the *difference* — obtainable by cheap simulation of the two predictive distributions, not by subtracting medians (invalid in general). If B fails to beat V1's median, the "two processes" hypothesis is substantially weakened for point forecasting, which is itself a publishable null — and the expensive I is then unwarranted.

### 3. G — Heteroskedastic location-scale margin model (mean + pregame conditional variance)
The formal version of the mandated Phase-2 variance-predictability diagnostic: fit μ(x), then model σ²(x) on pregame features, and test whether conditional variance is predictable out-of-sample. **Stated plainly for the protocol: this architecture cannot improve the MAE point forecast** — for symmetric errors the MAE-optimal median equals μ(x) regardless of σ(x); its win condition is Q(B) only (log-loss, interval calibration, cover-probability accuracy). That separation is exactly what 008 needs to avoid mistaking better uncertainty for better margin prediction. Cheap (~1.2× V1), no new data. If the diagnostic finds σ(x) unpredictable pregame, stop here and do not build F.

**Explicitly not recommended now:** C (structurally mis-specified for 7/3/2/1 scoring), E (pay the dependence-modeling cost only after Phase 2 measures nonzero home/away dependence), F (shape parameters too data-hungry at n≈3000 before G proves variance is predictable), I (large build; justified only if B's two-process split shows a Q(A) signal), J-literature models (premise-validating references, not new candidates).
